You post consistently. You try different formats. You ask a question at the end like everyone says to. The likes and comments stay flat, and someone (a boss, a client, or you) wants to know why.

The answer usually isn't that the content is bad. It's that engagement is downstream of something you haven't built yet: people who've already seen you be useful. A post asks a small audience to care from scratch, and most of the time they don't, because they've never heard of you.

Fixing that is less about writing better posts and more about where you spend the twenty minutes before you post.

## What counts as LinkedIn engagement

Engagement is what people do after seeing your content: reactions, comments, reposts, clicks, and on some views saves and sends. Profile visits often sit outside a post's engagement number, which is why a post can feel like it "did nothing" while three people quietly looked you up.

Worth knowing: the exact actions in a displayed engagement rate vary by which report you're looking at. LinkedIn's page analytics counts interactions like clicks, reactions, comments and shares. Check the definition before comparing two numbers, because people regularly compare rates that were calculated differently.

One more thing worth being precise about. A comment is a visible contribution; a like is nearly invisible to everyone except the author. That makes comments more useful for starting a conversation. It does not mean a comment is "worth three likes" to the ranking system, because LinkedIn hasn't published a weighting like that, and anyone quoting one is guessing.

## Why your engagement is flat

Three common reasons, roughly in order of how often they're the real problem.

**You're posting to an audience that doesn't exist yet.** Under about 1,000 relevant followers, every post is a cold start. That's not a content problem, it's a distribution problem.

**Your topic is too broad to be memorable.** "Lessons on leadership" is for everybody, which means it's for nobody. "What I learned running a 9-person sales team through a pricing change" is for about forty people and they'll all read it.

**Nobody has a reason to recognise you yet.** When someone sees a post from a name they don't know, the decision to engage takes a second of trust. Being a familiar name in the comments of posts they already read builds that trust far faster than publishing.

None of these are fixed by posting more.

## The tactics, honestly rated

| Tactic | Effort | What it actually gets you | Worth it? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Posting more | High | More attempts at a cold audience | Only if quality holds |
| Posting narrower | Medium | A reason for the right people to read and respond | Yes |
| Commenting on others' posts | Low | Visibility inside conversations your buyers already read | Yes, if the comment adds something |
| Replying to your own comments | Low | Threads that continue instead of dying | Yes |
| Asking a question in the post | Low | Occasional replies, often from the wrong people | Sometimes |
| Engagement pods | Medium | Numbers that look real to nobody | No |
| Tagging people | Low | A notification, sometimes irritation | Only when genuinely relevant |
| Comment-first strategy | Medium | Familiarity before you ask for attention | Yes, especially under 5,000 followers |

The pattern in that table is that the cheap tactics are the effective ones, and the expensive ones are the ones that don't work. Posting more is the most expensive and the least reliable.

## Engagement is downstream

You don't get engagement on your posts by wanting it more. You get it because people have already seen you being useful somewhere else.

That's why the comment-first approach works. When you add something worth reading under a post your buyers are already reading, three things happen at once: the author sees you, their audience sees you, and LinkedIn has a reason to think you're connected to that topic and those people. None of that requires you to own an audience.

Then when you do publish, some fraction of those people recognise the name and give the post its first honest engagement, which is the thing every post needs and nobody can fake convincingly.

## A weekly routine that works

This is deliberately small. Big routines collapse in week two.

**Each weekday, 15 minutes.** Find posts from people at your target companies or in the roles you care about. Read the full thread. Leave one or two comments that add something specific. If you have nothing to add, skip the post; a forced comment is worse than none.

**Twice a week, 5 minutes.** Check replies to your comments. Continue the ones worth continuing.

**Once a week, one post.** Write it for the narrow audience you've been commenting to. They already know you; now you're giving them something longer than a comment.

**Once a week, 10 minutes.** Look at who engaged. Not how many. Whether they're the right people, and whether any conversation continued off-platform.

## Engagement rate, and what "good" means

The common post-level formula:

**Engagement rate = engagements ÷ impressions × 100**

Twelve engagements on 600 impressions is 2%. That's the maths, not a benchmark.

There's no universal good number, and the people quoting one are usually quoting their own account. Rates vary enormously with audience size, format, industry and calculation method. An account with 40,000 followers and an account with 400 can post the same thing and get wildly different percentages, because small audiences are noisier.

Compare yourself only to yourself, over time, using the same formula. And pair the number with audience quality: 2% from people who could actually buy from you beats 8% from people who never will.

## What to stop doing

Stop writing "comment YES if you agree". Stop joining pods. Stop tagging people to borrow their audience. Stop posting to hit a quota when you have nothing to say.

And stop reading likes as pipeline. The only engagement number that matters is whether the people engaging could become customers, and that's a judgement you have to make by looking at names, not a percentage.

## FAQ

### How do I get more engagement on LinkedIn?

Publish narrower posts for a defined audience, and spend more time commenting in conversations where that audience is already active. Most flat engagement is a distribution problem, not a writing problem.

### Are comments better than likes on LinkedIn?

Comments create visible conversations with the author and other readers, while likes are nearly invisible. LinkedIn doesn't publish exact ranking weights, so treat claims that comments are worth a specific multiple of likes as guesses.

### What is a good LinkedIn engagement rate?

There isn't a universal one, because audiences, formats and calculation methods differ. Track your own posts with one consistent formula and judge the result by whether the people engaging are relevant.

### Does commenting on other posts increase engagement on my own?

It can, by making your name familiar to the people who later see your posts. It doesn't guarantee it. The value is recognition built before you ask for attention.

## The short version

Flat engagement usually isn't a content problem. It's that nobody knows you yet, and posting more doesn't fix that.

Pick ten companies or roles you want to reach. Spend fifteen minutes a day in the conversations they're already having. Publish once a week for that audience. Measure who shows up, not how many.
