You write something you're actually proud of, hit post, and watch it stall at 400 impressions. Then you start searching for the trick that fixes it, and find forty articles confidently explaining the posting time, hashtag number and self-comment ritual that will unlock distribution.

Most of that is astrology. Nobody outside LinkedIn knows the exact ranking model, and LinkedIn has never published a formula. What they have published is enough to tell you which levers are real and which are someone's guess dressed up as a rule.

Here's the honest version.

## What LinkedIn actually says

LinkedIn describes its feed in terms of relevance: how likely a given member is to care about a given piece of content. Their public explanations mention signals like the content itself, who posted it, how recent it is, the viewer's professional interests and past activity, and dwell time, meaning how long someone actually spends with a post.

That's genuinely useful direction. It is not a weight table, and no one claiming "comments are worth 3x likes" has a source. LinkedIn's own [overview of feed ranking](https://www.linkedin.com/help/linkedin/answer/a9554004) and their [writing on dwell time](https://www.linkedin.com/blog/engineering/feed/understanding-feed-dwell-time) are worth reading precisely because they're vague: they tell you what the system cares about, not how to score points against it.

## The popular advice, sorted

| What you'll read | What's actually true | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| "Post at 8am Tuesday" | Who's online matters, but there's no universal best hour | Test a few slots that fit your audience, compare over weeks |
| "Use exactly 3 hashtags" | Hashtags help label a topic; no magic number is documented | Use only tags that genuinely describe the post |
| "Links kill your reach" | No published rule says links are suppressed | Make the post worth reading without the click |
| "Comment on your own post to bump it" | A real author reply continues a conversation; a self-comment for distribution isn't verified | Reply when you have something to add |
| "Engagement pods work" | Coordinated low-relevance engagement can't manufacture genuine interest | Get responses from people who care about the topic |
| "More followers = more reach" | Followers are potential audience, not guaranteed distribution | Build a relevant network and show up where your audience already is |

The pattern: the advice that sticks around is the advice that describes a real behaviour, and the advice that gets sold hardest is the advice that promises you control over a model you can't see.

## What actually moves the needle

You can't control the ranker. You can control whether your activity is useful to a specific group of people, and whether they have a reason to respond.

**Relevance to someone specific.** "For everyone in business" gives nobody a reason to stop scrolling. "For B2B sales leaders whose pipeline is full of deals that never close" gives a hundred people a reason to read and ten a reason to reply.

**Conversation.** A post is one way to start a discussion. Replies in other people's threads are a way to join ones already running. Both feed the same thing: your name attached to a useful contribution.

**Consistency.** Not as a reach multiplier, because it isn't one. Consistency is how you accumulate evidence about what your audience responds to. Thirty posts tell you something twelve posts can't.

**Real early responses.** When people who actually care about the topic reply early, that says the post landed with its intended audience. LinkedIn doesn't publish what it does with that signal, and the "golden hour" framing is mostly folklore. But a post that gets responses from relevant people is, by definition, a post that's working.

## Why commenting is part of an algorithm strategy

Posting asks your existing audience to come to you. Commenting puts you in front of an audience that's already assembled, and it doesn't depend on you having followers.

When you leave a specific, useful comment on someone else's post, everyone in that thread sees your name next to it, including people who have never heard of you. Some will click through. Some will reply. Some will remember that you were the one who said the useful thing about their problem.

That's not a growth hack, it's just distribution from a different direction, and it compounds. Comment thoughtfully in the same twenty threads for a month and you become a familiar name to that specific audience, which is worth more than a viral post to an audience that doesn't buy anything.

The mechanics of a good comment matter here: it has to add something, prove you read the post, sound like a person, and invite a response. Generic praise takes up space and gives nobody a reason to remember you.

## What to measure instead of impressions

Impressions tell you a post appeared on screens. They don't tell you whether the right people saw it.

Look at signals connected to what you actually want:

- Are the people replying the kind of people you're trying to reach?
- Are comments turning into actual back-and-forth, or stopping at one exchange?
- Are profile visits, connection requests or follow-up conversations happening?
- Which topics reliably produce a useful response, and which produce likes and nothing else?
- Are people asking you the same question repeatedly? That's a post you should write.

A conversation with eight right people beats a post that got 14,000 impressions from strangers.

## What to stop doing

Stop treating every dip as proof the algorithm changed. Reach varies with topic, timing, audience and luck, and one post is never evidence of a penalty.

Stop posting to keep a streak. Stop adding hashtags that don't describe the post. Stop leaving "Great insight!" on things you didn't read. And stop building a strategy around an alleged secret formula, because LinkedIn's systems change and nobody outside the company can verify the claims.

## What to do this week

1. **Pick one audience.** Write down who you want to reach and the one problem they care about.
2. **Publish one post that speaks only to them.** Concrete, useful, readable without clicking anything.
3. **Join five conversations** where that audience is already talking. Add a thought, an example or a real question.
4. **Look at who showed up.** After a week, note who replied and what they cared about. That's your next post.

Nothing here guarantees a number of impressions. It gives you better evidence about what works with the people who matter, which is the only version of this you can control.

## FAQ

### How does the LinkedIn algorithm work?

LinkedIn ranks feed content by likely relevance to each member, using signals like the content itself, the poster, recency, and the viewer's interests and activity. It doesn't publish the full model or exact weights.

### Did the LinkedIn algorithm change in 2026?

LinkedIn's systems and features change regularly, but the ranking model isn't public. Treat claims about a specific new "secret rule" as unconfirmed unless LinkedIn explains it themselves.

### Do comments help you reach more people?

Comments can put your perspective in front of an audience that doesn't follow you, because threads are read by the author's audience as well as your own. LinkedIn doesn't publish how any individual comment affects ranking, so write for the people reading, not the model.

### Do you need to post every day?

No published rule says daily posting is required to grow. A sustainable schedule plus consistent participation in relevant conversations is a better starting point than posting to hit a quota.

The honest answer to "why did my post flop" is that nobody outside LinkedIn can tell you with certainty. What you can do is be more relevant to a narrower audience, show up in conversations that already have an audience, and pay attention to who responds. Start with one audience, one post, and five conversations this week.
